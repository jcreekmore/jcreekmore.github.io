I recently finished reading ["Religious Literacy: What Every American Needs to
Know -- And
Doesn't"](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0060859520?ie=UTF8&tag=jonathcreekm-20&linkCode=as2&camp=1789&creative=390957&creativeASIN=0060859520)![](http://www.assoc-
amazon.com/e/ir?t=jonathcreekm-20&l=as2&o=1&a=0060859520) by Stephen Prothero.
The book was primarily about the downfall of religious education in the United
States, mostly over the past century and especially among self-proclaimed
evangelical Christians. In it, he states that religion has become more about
"feeling" and that the basic knowledge of doctrine that leads to civil debate
among the different religions has been de-emphasized. The beginning of the
book lays out his argument for why the nation needs basic religious literacy,
the middle traces the downfall of religious literacy over the past four
centuries, and the book closes with a basic dictionary of religious terms that
everyone should know. I went into the book expecting to bolster my basic
religious literacy, but, alas, that was not really the focus of the book --
only a bit at the end. For that, I will have to wait until ["God Is Not One:
The Eight Rival Religions That Run the World -- and Why Their Differences
Matter"](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/006157127X?ie=UTF8&tag=jonathcreekm-20&linkCode=as2&camp=1789&creative=390957&creativeASIN=006157127X)![](http://www.assoc-
amazon.com/e/ir?t=jonathcreekm-20&l=as2&o=1&a=006157127X) becomes available at
the library. However, Stephen Prothero made two points within the first ten
pages that I thought enlightening -- specifically about religious literacy but
I think they are relevant to literacy in general -- so I wanted to repeat them
here. On page 4, he writes

> In my world religions classes I told my students that before we could
> discuss in any detail the great religious traditions of the world, we would
> need to have some shared vocabulary in each, some basic religious literacy.
> In this way, I became, like [E. D.] Hirsch, a traditionalist about content,
> not because I had come to see facts as the end of education but because I
> had come to see them as necessary means to understanding.

This comment aligns with the books I have been reading by Susan Wise Bauer
(["The Well-Trained
Mind"](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393067084?ie=UTF8&tag=jonathcreekm-20&linkCode=as2&camp=1789&creative=390957&creativeASIN=0393067084)![](http://www.assoc-
amazon.com/e/ir?t=jonathcreekm-20&l=as2&o=1&a=0393067084) and ["The Well-
Educated
Mind"](http://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393050947?ie=UTF8&tag=jonathcreekm-20&linkCode=as2&camp=1789&creative=390957&creativeASIN=0393050947)![](http://www.assoc-
amazon.com/e/ir?t=jonathcreekm-20&l=as2&o=1&a=0393050947)) about [Classical
Education](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_education_movement). The
classical education movement splits education into three phases called the
trivium: grammar, logic, and rhetoric. In each of the three stages, one
studies the same types of topics, but at different levels. In the grammar
level, the student is gaining basic facts -- a _shared vocabulary_ that the
student can use when she learns how to reason during the logic stage and
debate during the rhetoric stage. Following the trivium, you must strictly
move from one stage to the next in order and, without that basic foundation,
you cannot reason and debate intelligently on topics. While Prothero aimed his
comment toward religious literacy, it holds for education in general. On page
10, he writes

> [Religious] ignorance imperils our public life, putting citizens in the
> thrall of talking heads and effectively transferring power from the [third
> estate (the people)](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Estate#Third_Estate)
> to the [fourth (the press)](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Estate).

Once again, Prothero is talking about religious ignorance but I believe that
the quote holds for ignorance in general. Recently, I listened to an episode
of Dan Carlin's Common Sense called ["To Dum Two
Vowt"](http://www.dancarlin.com/disp.php/csarchive#Show-170---To-Dum-Two-
Vowt). In this episode, Carlin made explored what would happen if the United
States was too require literacy tests for voting -- he was not seriously
suggesting it, just exploring it from a hypothetical standpoint. Carlin makes
the same type of arguments as Prothero. Basically, he says that an ignorant
electorate is a dangerous electorate, although he ultimately comes to the
conclusion that requiring a basic literacy exam is too reminiscent of [Jim
Crow laws](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_crow) to be workable.
